Years of experience do not make someone senior. Five years of the same work is one year, five times.

What it means
The levels are not about how much code you know. They are about how big a problem you can own without someone checking on you.

Think of learning to cook. First you follow a recipe exactly. Then you cook a full meal alone. Later you plan the menu for a wedding and know which dishes can go wrong.

Why it matters
If you think seniority means knowing more frameworks, you will learn frameworks and stay stuck. The real change is in scope (how much you are trusted to own) and judgement.

What changes at each level
1. Junior: owns a task. Gets a clear ticket, asks for help, learns the codebase. Success means working code that passes review.
2. Mid-level: owns a feature. Turns a vague request into tasks, finishes without much help, and spots edge cases (the rare inputs that break things).
3. Senior: owns a problem or a system. Asks "should we build this at all?", sees risks early, and makes the whole team faster.

The same bug, three ways
Junior: fixes the line that crashed.
Mid: fixes the cause and adds a test.
Senior: asks why this kind of bug keeps happening, and changes the process or design so it stops.

What does not change
→ You still read code, debug and write tests every week.
→ You still ask questions. Seniors just ask them earlier.
→ Nobody knows everything at any level.

[PERSONAL: one moment you noticed your own scope grow, like the first time you pushed back on a feature instead of just building it]

For mid-levels aiming higher
Seniority is visible in the work you prevent. A senior who says "we do not need a new service, a database index is enough" saved weeks. Write those decisions down, because nobody sees work that did not happen.

Takeaway
Junior owns a task, mid owns a feature, senior owns the outcome.
